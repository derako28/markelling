For 17 years, his money ran through The Management Group, a firm owned by brothers Joel and Robert Mandel.

### Вариант 1
In 1990 the IRS told Willie Nelson he owed $16.7 million.

The tax shelters behind it were picked by his accountants at Price Waterhouse. The IRS threw out the deductions, and the bill went to the name on the return.

About $6 million of it was tax. More than $10 million was interest and penalties - the price of the years nobody looked.

On November 9, 1990, agents seized his property in six states. His golf club and recording studio, master tapes, gold records. His daughter got his guitar, Trigger, out before they came.

Then his friends started bidding. Darrell Royal, the Texas football coach, bought the country club to hold it for Willie. The IRS found out and cancelled the sale. A farmers' group Willie had helped through Farm Aid bought his ranch for the minimum bid, $203,840, so they could sell it back to him.

Willie sat down with one guitar and cut a double album, “The IRS Tapes: Who'll Buy My Memories?” You ordered it at 1-800-IRS-TAPE for $19.95. It raised $3.6 million for the IRS. He settled for $9 million in February 1993.

Years later he summed it up in one sentence: “I had some bad advice and took the advice and wound up owing the IRS a lot of money.”

The case for victim: he paid one of the biggest accounting firms in the world to get this right. He sued them for $45 million, and they settled.

The case for careless millionaire: the IRS doesn't bill the advisor. It bills the signature.

Here's the lesson. Nobody double-checks the advice that saves them money.

In the video, Willie plays himself in a 2003 H&R Block Super Bowl ad, forced into a shaving cream commercial to pay back taxes. Watch him, then decide.

Victim or careless millionaire?

### Вариант 2
Willie Nelson sold a $19.95 album to pay a $16.7 million tax bill.

The bill came from tax shelters his accountants at Price Waterhouse put him in. The IRS disallowed the deductions. By the time it came to collect, the interest and penalties - more than $10 million - were bigger than the roughly $6 million of tax underneath.

On November 9, 1990, agents seized his property in six states. Master tapes, gold records, a studio, a golf club.

The people around him all said the same thing. “Everyone was advising me to go bankrupt,” he said later. He refused, because it meant “cheating all those people out there you owed money to besides the IRS.”

So he took the opposite of the easy way out. Willie went in with one guitar and recorded a double album called “The IRS Tapes: Who'll Buy My Memories?” You could only get it by calling 1-800-IRS-TAPE. The government took its cut of every copy.

It raised $3.6 million. Not even a quarter of the bill. In February 1993 he settled with the IRS for $9 million.

The case for paying: it was his name on the returns, and he walked out owing nobody.

The case for walking away: the advice that created the debt wasn't his. He sued Price Waterhouse for $45 million, and they settled. The firm never had to sell a single record.

Here's the lesson. A tax shelter is someone else's assumption with your signature on it.

In the video, twelve years after the raid, Willie plays himself in an H&R Block Super Bowl ad, pushed into a shaving cream commercial to cover back taxes. Watch him, then decide.

Would you have paid it, or filed?
